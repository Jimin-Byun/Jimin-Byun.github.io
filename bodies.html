What this study shows

A summary of what moved across to the avatar and what did not.

Note This study was made with one phone camera and freely available pose-estimation tools. Within these limits the 3D reconstruction and its analysis remain partial: some limb errors and depth estimates could not be resolved.

Carried over

  • TimingEvery pose is placed at the moment it was recorded, so the phrasing stays intact.
  • TravelMovement across the floor is rebuilt from the fixed camera.

Not recorded

  • Spine and breathThere are no joints along the spine or on the chest; the bend between pelvis and chest is estimated.
  • Fingers and toesTwo points per hand and per foot can turn them but cannot bend them.

Limits

  • DepthIt is the model’s estimate from one camera. The side view shows where it is least reliable.
  • Hidden limbsSome errors remain where an arm or leg is hidden behind the body.
  • JumpsThey lose their height, because the lowest point of the body is kept on the floor.

Where the uncanny valley sits

Masahiro Mori (1970) proposed that our affinity for an artificial figure rises as it becomes more human-like, drops sharply just before full likeness, and that movement makes the drop deeper.

In an fMRI study, Ayse Pinar Saygin and colleagues (2012) found the strongest repetition suppression in the brain’s action-perception network for an android that looked human but moved mechanically, and traced it to the mismatch between appearance and motion. This study shows the reverse arrangement: human movement on figures that are not quite human.
